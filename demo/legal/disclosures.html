Consumer Credit File Rights Under State and Federal Law

This statement is provided because CREDIT/OS discusses credit repair. It is modeled on the disclosure required by 15 U.S.C. § 1679c. [ATTORNEY TO VERIFY WORDING AGAINST 15 U.S.C. § 1679c AND STATE REQUIREMENTS BEFORE PUBLICATION]

You have a right to dispute inaccurate information in your credit report by contacting the credit bureau directly. However, neither you nor any “credit repair” company or credit repair organization has the right to have accurate, current, and verifiable information removed from your credit report. The credit bureau must remove accurate, negative information from your report only if it is over 7 years old. Bankruptcy information can be reported for 10 years.

You have a right to obtain a copy of your credit report from a credit bureau or credit repair organization. You may be charged a reasonable fee for the report. There is no fee, however, if you have been turned down for credit, employment, insurance, or a rental dwelling because of information in your credit report within the preceding 60 days. The credit reporting agency must provide someone to help you interpret the information in your credit file. You are entitled to receive a free copy of your credit report if you are unemployed and intend to apply for employment in the next 60 days, if you are a recipient of public welfare assistance, or if you have reason to believe that there is inaccurate information in your credit report due to fraud.

You have a right to sue a credit repair organization that violates the Credit Repair Organization Act. This law prohibits deceptive practices by credit repair organizations.

You have the right to cancel your contract with any credit repair organization for any reason within 3 business days from the date you signed it.

Credit bureaus are required to follow reasonable procedures to ensure that the information they report is accurate. However, mistakes may occur.

You may, but are not required to, address your complaints about a credit bureau or credit repair organization directly with the credit bureau or credit repair organization. If you do so, the credit bureau or credit repair organization must let you know it received your complaint. It must also complete its investigation within 30 business days of receiving your complaint.

You may also file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (www.consumerfinance.gov) and the Federal Trade Commission (www.ftc.gov) about a credit bureau or credit repair organization.

Your federal rights (Fair Credit Reporting Act)

  • Free reports. You can get a free report from each nationwide bureau every 12 months at AnnualCreditReport.com.
  • Disputes. You can dispute information directly with a bureau (15 U.S.C. § 1681i) or with the company that reported it (12 CFR § 1022.43). The bureau generally has 30 days to investigate, or 45 days if you send more information during that time.
  • Verification. If information is found to be inaccurate, incomplete or cannot be verified, it must be corrected or deleted. You can ask how a disputed item was verified.
  • Identity theft. If you are a victim, you can file a report at IdentityTheft.gov and ask the bureaus to block the fraudulent information (15 U.S.C. § 1681c-2).
  • Lawsuits. You can sue for violations of the FCRA. Attorneys often take these cases where the company pays fees if you win.

Things we want you to know

  • Accurate negative information generally stays on your report for up to 7 years from the date the delinquency began. Bankruptcies can stay for up to 10 years.
  • Paying a debt does not remove it from your report unless the creditor agrees to delete it or the item expires.
  • Score results vary by scoring model and by each bureau. No tool can promise a score change.
  • Scoring models. Lenders use different scores (for example FICO and VantageScore versions). Some newer scores and buy-now-pay-later data may not be included on every report.
  • Medical debt. The federal medical-debt reporting rule was vacated by a court in July 2025. Some bureaus have their own voluntary policies, and some states have their own protections. Check your state.
  • State law. Many states add their own credit and collection protections. Some states regulate credit repair businesses and require registration or a bond. Check your state attorney general’s website.
